Bits, Characters & Encoding

Computers do not speak English, Spanish, or Hindi; they only understand micro-switches. Before you can scramble a message, you must understand how thoughts turn into raw digital material.

1. From Switches to Characters

At the atomic level, all digital data is made of two states:

  • Bit: A single 0 or 1 (a physical microscopic switch: off or on).
  • Byte: A bundle of 8 bits. An 8-bit byte can represent $2^8 = 256$ different combinations (numbers from 0 to 255).

In the 1960s, engineers standardized ASCII (American Standard Code for Information Interchange). It is simply a dictionary mapping numbers to English letters. For example, the capital letter A is number 65 (`01000001` in binary).

As the internet grew global, Unicode (UTF-8) was born to give a unique number to every letter, accent, and emoji in every human language.

3. Hexadecimal & The Mystery of Base64 "=" Padding

Why do cryptographers love Hexadecimal (Base16)? Binary is too long for humans to read (`01001111` is dizzying). Hex counts from 0-9 and a-f. Exactly two hex characters represent one full 8-bit byte. Whenever you see a hash like e3b0c442..., each pair of letters is just one byte!

Base64 solves a different problem: old email protocols and web URLs crash if you send raw binary bytes. Base64 groups 3 bytes (24 bits) into 4 chunks of 6 bits, representing them using 64 safe characters (`A-Z`, `a-z`, `0-9`, `+`, `/`).

Why the = signs? Try typing Test (4 bytes) in the inspector above. You will see VGVzdA==. Because 4 bytes doesn't divide evenly into groups of 3, the computer pads the empty bits with zeroes and appends == to tell the recipient: "These trailing bytes are just spacers!"

4. The Cardinal Law: Encoding $\neq$ Encryption

Crucial Industry Rule

Encoding is publicly reversible with zero password. It is used strictly for data compatibility. Anyone can paste Base64 into a browser and decode it in 1 millisecond. Never store confidential passwords or credit cards using Base64.
