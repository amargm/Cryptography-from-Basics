Digital Signatures, PKI & Trust

Public keys let you encrypt messages, but how do you know the public key actually belongs to your bank, and not to a hacker impersonating your bank?

1. Turning Asymmetric Keys Upside Down

In public-key encryption, we encrypt with the public key and decrypt with the private key.

What happens if you reverse the process?

The Digital Signature Recipe

Step 1: Alice hashes her contract using SHA-256 (creating a unique 32-byte fingerprint).
Step 2: Alice encrypts that fingerprint using her Private Key. This encrypted hash is her Digital Signature.
Step 3: Anyone in the world can decrypt the signature using Alice's Public Key. If the decrypted hash matches the document, it proves beyond doubt: Only Alice could have created it, and not a single letter was altered!

2. The Man-in-the-Middle (MITM) Disaster

Public keys solve key exchange, but they do NOT solve identity.

Man-in-the-Middle Simulator Interactive Explainer
1. Alice sends her Public Key
Alice says: "Hi, use my Key [A] to talk to me!"
Origin
2. Mallory Intercepts & Swaps Keys!
Mallory drops Key [A] and forwards her own Key [M] to Bob!
Attacker
3. Bob receives Key [M] thinking it is Alice
Bob encrypts his password with Key [M]. Mallory decrypts and reads everything!
Vulnerable!

3. The Solution: PKI & Digital Certificates

How does Bob know Key [A] really belongs to Alice?

The solution is Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) and Digital Certificates (X.509):

  • The Passport Analogy: When you travel internationally, border control doesn't just believe your handwritten name badge; they check your government-issued passport, verified by a trusted authority seal.
  • Certificate Authority (CA): Globally trusted institutions (like Let's Encrypt or DigiCert) whose root certificates are pre-installed into your smartphone and operating system.
  • The Certificate: When your browser connects to google.com, Google presents a certificate signed by a root CA saying: "We verify that this public key belongs to Google."
