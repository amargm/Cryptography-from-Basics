The Core Philosophy

Cryptography is not the art of secret languages; it is the mathematical science of trust in a world where everyone might be listening.

1. The Cast of Characters

In 1978, computer scientists Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman got tired of writing "User A wants to send data to User B while Adversary C listens." They invented the cast of characters used in every security textbook on Earth:

  • Alice & Bob: The legitimate communicators. They could be your phone talking to your bank's server.
  • Eve (The Eavesdropper): A passive listener. Eve sits in the middle (e.g. coffee shop public Wi-Fi) reading everything, but she does not tamper with the data.
  • Mallory (The Malicious Attacker): An active adversary. Mallory doesn't just listen; she deletes messages, alters payment amounts, and injects fake packets.
Alice's Plaintext View:
"Approve wire transfer: $1,200 to Bob"
On Alice's physical screen, the message is readable text. Before leaving her device, it must be encrypted.

2. The Four Pillars of Trust

Most people think cryptography is only about keeping things secret. Secrecy is only one quarter of the puzzle. Modern systems solve four distinct problems:

The 4 Pillars Breakdown

1. Confidentiality: Only intended recipients can read the message.
2. Integrity: Proof that nobody altered a single byte during transit.
3. Authenticity: Proof of who actually created the message.
4. Non-Repudiation: The sender cannot later lie and claim: "I never sent that!"

Notice how you can have integrity without confidentiality! If a government publishes a new tax law, it doesn't need to be secret (no confidentiality), but citizens must know for sure that no hacker modified the numbers (integrity & authenticity).

3. Kerckhoffs’s Principle: The Golden Rule

In 1883, military cryptographer Auguste Kerckhoffs stated the most important rule in security engineering:

"A cryptosystem should be secure even if everything about the system, except the key, is public knowledge."

Why? Because if your security depends on keeping the lock mechanism secret ("Security through Obscurity"), you are ruined the moment one disgruntled employee leaks the code or a reverse-engineer inspects the app.

In modern cryptography, algorithms like AES-256 and RSA are completely public. Millions of researchers scrutinize them daily. The security lives 100% inside the secret key.
