Classical Ciphers & Breaking Patterns

For thousands of years, humans believed that swapping or reordering letters was enough to hide secrets. They overlooked one fatal flaw: human language leaves unmistakable footprints.

1. The Caesar Shift: The Oldest Trick

Julius Caesar did not trust his messengers. When writing to his generals, he shifted every letter in the Roman alphabet forward by 3 positions: A became D, B became E, and so on.

Because the English alphabet has only 26 letters, there are only 25 possible keys. Even without a computer, a Roman soldier could try all 25 shifts on a napkin in 3 minutes.

2. Frequency Analysis: The Death of Substitution

To defeat simple shift guessing, scholars invented Monoalphabetic Substitution, mapping letters randomly (e.g., A $\to$ X, B $\to$ Q). This yields $26! \approx 4 \times 10^{26}$ combinations. A human could never guess them all.

Yet in the 9th century, Arab mathematician Al-Kindi cracked it using one profound insight: Letter swapping scrambles the symbols, but preserves the rhythm of language.

The Inescapable Fingerprint of English

In any typical English book, the letter E appears about 13% of the time. The letters T (9%) and A (8%) follow closely. The letters Q and Z appear less than 0.2% of the time.

If a hacker counts the letters in an intercepted ciphertext and finds that W appears 13% of the time, W is almost certainly the letter E!

3. The One-Time Pad: The Only Unbreakable Cipher

In 1917, engineers discovered the only mathematically proven unbreakable cipher: the One-Time Pad (OTP).

If you take a message and combine each letter with a completely random letter from a key that is used only once and never reused, the resulting ciphertext has zero statistical patterns. Even an infinite supercomputer cannot crack it because every possible plaintext is equally likely.

Why don't we use it for everything? The Key Distribution Problem: The secret key must be just as long as the message. If you want to download a 50 GB video game securely, you would need to securely transport a 50 GB random key first!
